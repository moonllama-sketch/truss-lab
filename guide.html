The whole idea

One number per member

A truss is a set of straight bars, pinned at their ends, loaded only at those pins. Take any one bar out and look at it on its own: it has forces at two points and nowhere else. A body held by exactly two forces can only balance if they are equal, opposite, and along the line joining them.

So every member does one of two things. It pulls its two joints together, or it pushes them apart. Nothing else is available to it — no shear, no bending, no twist. One signed number says everything there is to say about a member, and that is what makes a truss with fifty bars something you can do by hand.

Assume tension. Every member. Every time.

Draw each unknown as an arrow pointing away from the joint, along the member. Then solve. A positive answer means the member really does pull — tension. A negative answer means it pushes — compression — and you do not redraw anything. The minus sign is the answer.

This is not a stylistic preference. Students who guess the sense at each joint and flip arrows to suit lose a sign somewhere around the fourth joint, and the error then travels through every member downstream of it. Students who assume tension do not.

Two idealisations pay for all this: the joints are frictionless pins, and the members are weightless. Real gusset plates are welded and real steel is heavy. The errors are small, they are conservative in the ways that matter, and the analysis you get is the one every code of practice is written around.

Counting

Count before you compute

Each joint is a set of concurrent forces, so it gives you two equations — ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 — and no moment equation at all, because every force at a joint passes through the same point. That is 2j equations. The unknowns are one force per member plus the reaction components.

m + r vs 2jWhat you haveWhat to do
m + r = 2jStatically determinate. Equilibrium alone fixes every bar force.Carry on. Nothing about the material, the member sizes or the sag can enter.
m + r > 2jIndeterminate. Redundant members, or extra supports.Statics cannot finish it. You need compatibility — a later course.
m + r < 2jA mechanism. Too few bars to hold the shape.It folds up. Not a structure at all.

The lab does this count for you and prints the verdict in the header of the Truss card. It takes ten seconds by hand and it tells you whether the method you are about to spend twenty minutes on can work at all.

Counting equal is necessary but not sufficient. The bars still have to be arranged sensibly. Drag a joint in the lab until three members become collinear and the count still reads m + r = 2j while the truss quietly becomes a mechanism — the header switches to mechanism and the worked solution says why. That is worth doing once on purpose.

Method of
joints

Two equations, so two unknowns

Isolate one joint. The members pull or push on it along their own lines, the load and the reaction act on it too, and the lot must balance. Two equations — which settle two unknowns and not one more.

So the march cannot start anywhere you like. It has to begin at a joint where only two members are still unknown, which in practice means a support joint. Once that joint is solved, its member forces are known at the far end too, which drops the unknown count at the next joint along. Solve, carry forward, repeat.

Notice the order the equations came out in. ΣFy was used first, not because it comes first in the book, but because only one of the two unknowns has a vertical component. Look for that at every joint: the equation with one unknown in it is the one to write down.

Near the end, joints start giving you checks

As the march proceeds you will reach joints where only one member is still unknown. One equation settles it; the other is then a free check that costs nothing. The lab prints it as left over and shows it closing on zero. When yours does not close, the mistake is upstream — at a joint you did earlier, not the one that failed.

Zero-force
members

Some bars carry nothing

Two rules find them by inspection, before any algebra. Both come from resolving in a cleverly chosen direction, and both take one line.

Rule 1 needs a joint where only two members meet, which is rare once a truss has any depth — nearly every interior joint here has three or more. You can manufacture one: load the Triangle preset, then set its load to zero, and both members at the apex go quiet.

Zero does not mean useless, and it does not mean permanent. A zero-force member is zero for this load case. It still braces its neighbours against buckling, it carries load the moment the loading changes, and it is often the only reason the truss is not a mechanism. Never rub one out of the drawing — and never leave it out of the count.

Once you have found one, look again: a member known to be zero can turn its neighbour into a two-member joint, and the rules fire a second time. That is why the lab's count of zero-force members is sometimes larger than the number the two rules catch on the first pass.

Method of
sections

One cut, one member

Suppose you want the bottom chord at midspan and nothing else. The march would grind through half the truss to reach it. Instead, cut the truss in two and throw one half away.

Replace each severed bar by its force — pulling away from the body, as tension, exactly as at a joint — and what is left is an ordinary rigid body in equilibrium. And a rigid body has three equations, not two: ΣFx, ΣFy and ΣM about any point you please. That extra moment equation is the whole advantage, and it is why the rule is cut at most three members whose force you do not already know.

Choosing the moment centre is the whole method

It is the same instinct as taking moments about a support to kill a reaction, only now you are killing bar forces. For any one of the three, take moments about the point where the lines of the other two intersect. Both pass through it, both have zero arm, both disappear.

When the other two are parallel

Cut a flat truss and the two chords you sever are parallel. Their lines never meet, so for the diagonal there is no moment centre to be had. Sum forces perpendicular to them instead — vertically — and both chords drop out just as cleanly, because neither has a vertical component.

In the cut above that reads −3.75 + 0.600 FL2U3 = 0, so FL2U3 = +6.25 kN. Read the equation again and it says something worth remembering: the diagonal carries the shear on the section. That is why leaning the diagonals the other way — a Howe instead of a Pratt — flips every one of them from tension to compression.

Either half will do. The cut members must hold whichever portion you keep in equilibrium all by themselves, and both halves give the same three forces, because each pair of internal forces is equal and opposite. Keep the side with fewer forces on it; that is the only reason to prefer one. The Keep left / right switch in the lab rewrites the entire solution so you can watch the two agree.

Presets

Triangle and King post are small enough to do in your head. Pratt and Howe are the same bars with the interior diagonals leaning opposite ways — press one then the other and watch every diagonal change sign. Warren drops the verticals for a single zig-zag. Roof pitches the top chord to an apex. Custom hands you whatever is on screen to edit by hand, and Random shuffles the lot.

Pratt, Howe and Roof need an even number of panels — with an odd count the middle bay would take two diagonals and the truss would stop being determinate. The lab rounds you up to the next even number rather than building something broken.

Units and scale

The button reading kN · m switches to lb · ft. Only the labels change — the arithmetic is identical. Load and reaction arrows are drawn to their own force scale, shown by the bar at the foot of the diagram; a 50 kN arrow is not “50 metres long”. Distances come off the ruler along the top and the dimension bars.

Building
your own

Draw a truss and watch it decide

Press Build above the diagram and the stage becomes an editor. The last step of the walkthrough drops you straight into it with a rectangle to fix. There are only three gestures, and they compose into everything:

  • Click empty space — drops a joint there. If a joint is already picked up, the new one arrives already joined to it, so you can run a chain of members out in one series of clicks.
  • Click a joint, then a second joint — joins them if they are not joined, and unjoins them if they are. The same gesture builds and demolishes.
  • Drag a joint — moves it, exactly as in the presets. A press that does not travel is a click; one that travels is a drag.

The buttons in the Build card act on the joint you last clicked: put the pin or the roller on it, hang a load from it, or delete it along with every member meeting it. A joint carrying a support will not delete until you have moved that support somewhere else — the truss is never allowed to lose a reaction by accident.

The count is live, and it counts down

As you work, the card keeps a running verdict: m + r = 15 against 2j = 16 — 1 more member would balance the count. Add that member and it flips to determinate. Add one more and it tells you the truss is now indeterminate and statics alone can no longer finish it.

This is the counting rule from the top of this guide, turned into something you can feel. Build a rectangle of four bars, shove its top corner sideways, and it will tell you it is one short — nothing in it resists the shove, and the four bars simply lean over into a parallelogram. Join two opposite corners and the count settles, the diagonal picks up the whole shove, and the rectangle has become two triangles. That is why trusses are made of triangles, and why every braced frame in every building has a diagonal in it — demonstrated rather than asserted.

Watch for the verdict that says the count balances but the geometry does not. Put three members in a straight line through one joint, or stack the two supports at the same station, and the counting rule still reads m + r = 2j while the frame quietly becomes a mechanism. The lab says so in as many words.

That case is worth building on purpose. It is the proof that counting members is necessary but not sufficient, and it is much more convincing when you have just built it yourself than when a textbook says so in a footnote.

Method

Working a problem

  1. Count

    m + r against 2j. If they do not match, stop — no amount of algebra will help.

  2. Reactions, from the whole truss

    Treat the truss as one rigid body. ΣMA = 0 gives By, then ΣFy = 0 gives Ay, and ΣFx = 0 gives Ax. The internal arrangement of the bars makes no difference here at all — swap the web for a solid plate and the reactions would be identical.

    Everything downstream is measured from these numbers, so an error here quietly wrecks the entire truss. Check them the other way round before you go on.

  3. Sweep for zero-force members

    Two rules, one line each, before any algebra. Then look again — a member found to be zero can turn its neighbour into a two-member joint.

  4. Decide which method the question deserves

    One or two members wanted, somewhere in the middle? Sections. The whole truss? Joints. In practice you mix them: a section to break into the middle, then joints marching outward from there.

  5. If joints: start at a support and march

    Find a joint with two unknown members. Draw both as tension. Write the equation that has one unknown in it first — usually the one perpendicular to the other member. Carry each answer forward to the far end of its bar and move on.

  6. If sections: cut three, take moments where two meet

    Cut through a bay, not through a joint. Keep the side with fewer forces on it. For the member you want, find where the lines of the other two intersect and take moments there. If those two are parallel chords, sum forces perpendicular to them instead.

  7. Read the signs, then check

    Positive is tension, negative is compression, and neither is an error. Verify with an equation you have not used: a leftover joint equation, or the same member solved by the other method.

Practice

Getting marked

The Practice tab locks the truss and generates a fresh problem. Everything you need is in the paragraph at the top of the card — the truss type, the span, the depth, the panel count, which joints carry what. Read it from there, not off the picture; the diagram is a sketch to think against, with dimensions on and every force hidden. Zoom and pan still work.

Enter or Check marks your answers; a tick or a cross appears in each box, so you can see exactly where a chain of work went wrong rather than just that it did. Your score sits in the card header, and only your first attempt at each problem counts — press Show solution whenever you want the full working, which then appears beneath the buttons in the same format as the Explore tab.

Every member-force box takes a signed number with tension positive, so a compression member is entered as a negative. The lab will not accept a magnitude with the sign left off, and that is deliberate: the sign is half of what a bar force tells you. Reaction boxes are upward-positive as usual, and x of O is an ordinary coordinate measured from the left-hand support.

The two sections problems are the ones worth slowing down for, because each has a step before the answer that is easy to skip. The chord problem asks where O is before it asks for the force — if you cannot name that point, you are not using the method, you are guessing at it. The diagonal problem asks for the net vertical force on the kept portion first, which is the shear, and then makes you resolve it onto a sloping bar.

One more it will point out even when you are right: a negative member force is an answer, not an error. It means the bar is a strut rather than a tie, and that distinction is the reason a Pratt truss is the usual choice in steel — its interior diagonals go into tension under ordinary downward load, and a slender tie cannot buckle while a slender strut can.

The other error the lab will not let you make quietly is starting the march at a joint with three unknown members. Two equations cannot settle three forces, however long you stare at them, and if no joint anywhere has fewer than three the worked solution says so outright — that is precisely the situation the method of sections was invented for.

The two section formulas are the same equation twice. When the other two cut members meet at a point, kill them with a moment; when they are parallel and never meet, kill them with a force sum perpendicular to both. Either way you are choosing the equation in which two of your three unknowns are not present — which, once you have seen it, is the only idea in this entire topic.

Loads act at joints only, members are pin-jointed and weightless, and every member is therefore a two-force member carrying force along its own axis. Member forces are signed with tension positive. Up and counter-clockwise are positive; angles are measured from the +x axis. Every figure in the lab is computed from exact values and rounded only for display, so a rounded intermediate step may differ from the final one in the last digit — do not chase it. If your answer disagrees with the lab in the second decimal place, check your rounding before you check your method.
